What design teams grapple with today

  1. Redrawing before designing. The customer sends a PDF of a bare steel shell. A designer redraws it in CAD and reads every diameter, height and opening by hand before any lining decision.
  2. Judgement kept in personal files. Each zone of a converter or ladle wears differently and needs its own grade and thickness. Those choices come from individual experience, Excel sheets and past designs on individual PCs.
  3. Every course solved by hand. The tapered-brick mix and key brick are worked out for each ring, the bottom is laid out ring by ring, and quantities are counted at the end.
  4. Checks late, rework often. Volume, freeboard and shell temperature are checked after the layout. The drawings and BOM go back and forth with production and site teams before an offer is ready.
The Zero Zeta position

A lining design is engineering. The arithmetic around it should not take three weeks.

Choosing a zone, a grade or a thickness is judgement, and it stays with the designer. Turning those choices into thousands of correctly counted bricks is geometry. We automate the geometry, keep the judgement visible and editable, and check the whole vessel every time a value changes. The designer spends the time on the decisions that wear a lining out early, not on redrawing a shell.

Operating context

Why a lining offer takes weeks

Your designers are not slow. A converter lining is a few hundred independent calculations, and today most of them are done once, by hand, late in the job.

~86 × 2

A 180 t converter wall has about 86 brick courses. Each course in a cone has a different radius, so each needs its own pair of equations to find how many A and B tapered bricks close the ring, plus a cut key brick. Change one thickness and every course above it changes.

Counted from a reference 180 t BOF lining with 100 mm courses. Barrel courses repeat; cone courses do not.

1,199

The bottom of the same vessel is 1,199 bricks in 16 rings, with cut pieces wherever a ring crosses one of 10 stirring elements. A miscount here surfaces at site, when the vessel is down and every hour of relining is lost production.

This is arithmetic, not a survey. The counts follow from the dish radius, ring width and element layout on the drawing.

The challenge

Three places where lining design loses time

None of these are failures of skill. They are the limits of designing in CAD and spreadsheets that do not talk to each other.

01

Redrawing before designing

The customer sends a PDF of a bare shell. Before any lining decision, a designer redraws it in CAD and reads off diameters, heights, cone angles, taphole and stirring layout by hand.

02

Know-how kept in personal files

Zone templates, wear rates, standard brick lengths and closing pairs live in each designer's spreadsheets and past jobs. Two designers can produce two different linings for the same vessel.

03

Checks done at the end

Specific volume, freeboard, shell temperature and lining weight are checked after the brickwork is laid out. A failed check sends the design back to the start.

Workflow integration

How the copilot fits the design office

Four stages, each closed by a named confirmation. Nothing moves forward until the designer accepts it.

Stage 01

Read the shell drawing

Vector PDF, DXF or DWG. Diameters, elevations, cone angles, taphole and stirring layout are extracted with a confidence score; low-confidence values are flagged for review.

Designer confirms each section
Stage 02

Set zones and the lining concept

Height zones, angular spans, grades, permanent lining and working thickness from target life and wear rate. The vessel is checked as a whole before any brick is laid.

Designer sets judgement values
Stage 03

Lay out the brickwork

Bottom rings, every wall course, junctions, taphole and stirring assemblies, mouth. Each course is closed with tapered pairs and a cut key, with expansion allowances applied.

Computed, shown with worked values
Stage 04

Check, approve, issue

Every validation in one list, BOM by zone, mark and grade with tonnes, and a full audit trail of what changed and why. Export to Excel and your drawing set.

Under the hood

Computed where it can be, suggested where it must be

Ring closure, volumes and counts are deterministic geometry. Zone templates and wear rates are engineering judgement. The copilot keeps the two apart and shows which is which.

Ring closure for one course

A-count × A-hot  + B-count × B-hot  = 2π × hot radius
A-count × A-cold + B-count × B-cold = 2π × cold radius

pair A 140→160 mm · B 110→190 mm · hot radius 2.5 m
→ 70.5 A + 53.1 B ≈ 124 bricks, round down, cut key ≥ minimum

All-A closes a ring of about 6.3 m radius, all-B about 1.2 m. Mixing the two closes any radius in between, which is why one pair can serve a whole cone.

Technical boundaries, stated up front. The first release handles axisymmetric vessels with standard features: taphole, stirring elements, trunnions, mouth ring. Shell temperature is a one-dimensional estimate, not a finite-element model. Wear rates come from your data or engineering defaults; predicting wear from operating practice needs campaign history and laser wear scans. Asymmetric and free-form 3-D shells are planned for a later phase.

Why did the charge pad come out thicker than the tap pad on this job?

Copilot

The charge pad wear rate is 0.125 mm/heat against 0.115 on the trunnion and tap pad, because this customer charges 18% heavy scrap by chute. At a target of 6,000 heats plus 150 mm residual, the charge pad needs 900 mm and the tap pad 850 mm, the nearest standard lengths. Expected lives are within the 15% balance tolerance. On two earlier vessels of this size the charge pad was the first zone to reach residual. Want the life comparison, or a version with the charge pad in a higher hot-strength grade?
